Transformer-Enhanced SAC for Joint
Power–Frequency Allocation in HTS Systems
Abstract—In future space–air–ground integrated networks
(SAGIN), multi-beam high-throughput satellite (HTS) systems
require efficient joint allocation of power and frequency resources
under time-varying scenario to accommodate growing demand
and enhance overall system capacity. To address the challenges
of high-dimensional dynamic resource management, this paper
proposes a Transformer-enhanced Soft Actor–Critic (T-SAC)
algorithm. The resource allocation problem is formulated as
a Satellite-Centric Markov Decision Process (SC-MDP), and a
multi-head self-attention Transformer encoder is incorporated
into the SAC framework as a global feature extractor to ef-
fectively capture cross-beam spatio-temporal dependencies and
alleviate severe co-channel interference. The proposed method
achieves superior higher sample efficiency, training stability, and
stronger generalization in high-dimensional mixed action spaces.
Simulation results show that T-SAC achieves significant improve-
ments over baseline algorithms in average signal-to-interference-
plus-noise ratio (SINR), satisfaction-gap–based fairness (SGM),
and overall user satisfaction, demonstrating its robustness and
efficiency in dynamic multi-beam environments.
Index Terms —High-throughput satellite, dynamic resource
allocation, deep reinforcement learning, Soft Actor–Critic, trans-
former
I. I NTRODUCTION
Multi-beam high-throughput satellite (HTS) systems have
become a key enabler of future space–air–ground integrated
networks (SAGIN). Through frequency reuse and flexible
payload architectures, HTS systems can significantly enhance
spectral efficiency and overall system capacity [1]–[3]. How-
ever, as the number of beams and user demand continue
to grow, the joint allocation of power and frequency under
time-varying scenarios has evolved into a complex NP-hard
problem [4]. Geographically heterogeneous traffic distribu-
tions and severe co-channel interference further increase this
complexity and jointly determine system throughput and user
satisfaction. Consequently, intelligent, scalable, and adaptive
resource allocation has become a central research challenge
for next-generation satellite communication systems.
Early research primarily relied on convex optimization and
mathematical programming approaches. Typical works formu-
lated convex optimization models and employed sequential
convex approximation (SCA) to maximize system through-
put or minimize total power consumption under signal-to-
interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) and bandwidth constraints
[3]. Although these methods can yield theoretically optimal
or near-optimal solutions in small-scale geostationary orbit
(GEO) systems, they depend heavily on accurate channel
information and centralized computation. As the number
of beams increases, their computational complexity grows
rapidly, limiting scalability and making them unsuitable for
real-time HTS operations [2]. To alleviate the burden imposed
by large beam counts, meta-heuristic algorithms—such as
genetic algorithms (GA) [5], simulated annealing (SA) [6], and
particle swarm optimization (PSO) [7]—have been introduced
for joint resource allocation. However, these methods often
suffer from slow convergence, sensitivity to initialization, and
limited adaptability to time-varying environments.
In recent years, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has
emerged as a promising tool for tackling complex, dynamic
satellite environments. Unlike model-driven methods, DRL
does not require an explicit system model; instead, it learns
resource allocation policies through interactions with the envi-
ronment [1], [6], [8]–[11], and has demonstrated performance
superior to convex optimization and heuristic algorithm in
dynamic multi-beam scenarios. However, when these classical
DRL algorithms are applied directly to HTS systems with
hundreds of beams, the mixed power–frequency action space
grows exponentially. This results in severely reduced explo-
ration efficiency, sparse rewards, and unstable training, causing
slow convergence or entrapment in suboptimal solutions. To
enhance scalability, multi-agent DRL (MADRL) frameworks
have been introduced to perform distributed joint decision-
making [2], [12], [13]. Nonetheless, under constrained on-
board computation and non-stationary multi-agent interactions,
MADRL still suffers from heavy communication and training
overhead, and limited convergence stability.Against the above
limitations of traditional DRL methods, the Soft Actor–Critic
(SAC) algorithm [14] offers higher sample efficiency and
superior training stability, making it naturally well-suited for
constructing a high-dimensional mixed-action resource alloca-
tion framework for multi-beam HTS systems.
On the other hand, existing DRL-based satellite resource al-
location frameworks also face architectural limitations. Main-
stream network architecture such as multilayer perceptron
(MLP) and convolutional neural network (CNN) primarily
extract local features and struggle to model global correlations
among beams. As a result, their collaboration and generaliza-
tion capability deteriorate significantly when traffic distribu-
tion or beam counts change. Transformer has recently emerged
as a promising alternative [15]. By exploiting multi-head self-
attention, Transformers can explicitly capture global and long-
range spatio-temporal dependencies. As demonstrated in [16],
Transformer-based models exhibit superior policy stability,
exploration efficiency, and generalization in large action spaces
compared with conventional deep neural networks.
This paper proposes a Transformer-enhanced Soft Actor-

Critic algorithm (T-SAC) for multi-beam HTS systems, de-
signed for joint power-frequency resource allocation. The
resource management problem is modeled as a Satellite-
Centric Markov Decision Process (SCMDP). The proposed

II. S YSTEM MODEL AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. System Model
Consider the downlink of a time-slotted, multi-beam, HTS
communication system with a single GEO satellite. Let T =
{1, 2, . . . , T} denote the set of time slot indices. The on-board
beam-coverage schedule [17] is updated every ∆t time units,
so each time slot spans exactly one update interval. As shown
in Fig.1, the multi-beam satellite can generate Nb beams,
which are denoted as B = {1, 2, . . . , Nb} with the beamwidths
of bw◦. To reduce modeling complexity and highlight the
core mechanism of the dynamic resource allocation strategy,
we assume that within each cell determined by the beam-
coverage schedule, the traffic demand of all users is rep-
resented by a superuser located at the beam-pointing cen-
ter. Consequently, Nb cells are illuminated by these beams.
Therefore, the traffic demand of the cell can be expressed
as D(t) = {td1(t), td2(t), . . . , tdNb(t)} in which tdb(t) is the
aggregated traffic demand of cell b at time t. Correspondingly,
the actual traffic allocated by the satellite communication
system is denoted as A(t) = {tp1(t), tp2(t), . . . , tpNb(t)} in
which tpb(t) denotes the actual traffic allocated to cell b at
time t.

IV. S IMULATION RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In this section, several simulation experiments are carried
out to verify the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm.
First, a multi-beam High Throughput Satellite (HTS) commu-
nication system simulation environment is established using
Python, and a reinforcement learning framework is imple-
mented in PyTorch, where an agent interacts with the environ-
ment to generate joint power–frequency allocation strategies.
In the simulation setup, the HTS beams cover 100 major cities
across China. The traffic demand for each beam is randomly
generated, and the beams are indexed in descending order
according to their traffic demands to simulate the uneven load
distribution across regions. The main simulation parameters
and the main T-SAC parameters are summarized in Table 1
and Table 2, respectively.
To ensure the fairness and comprehensiveness of the evalu-
ation, this paper implements seven typical baseline algorithms
under the same simulation conditions, as follows:
1) Fixed Scheme: Each beam is assigned a fixed number
of FS and a constant transmit power, with the starting FS and
FG selected sequentially.
2) Greedy Scheme: In each allocation, the algorithm selects
the FS with the least occupied beam based on the current FS
occupancy status. The transmit power is adaptively selected
from three levels P T x
b (t) ∈ { 20W, 25W, 30W } based on
traffic demand.
3) Random Scheme: By uniformly sampling frequency and
power combinations in the legal action space, serving as a
reference for the lower limit of performance.
4) MLP-SAC: SAC based on multilayer perception (MLP).
5) CNN-SAC: SAC based on convolutional neural networks
(CNN).
6) MLP-DQN: Deep Q-Network (DQN) based on MLP. It
employs a factorized three-head design to separately estimate
Q-values for the discrete action spaces of frequency, time-
slot, and power. Each branch independently predicts the action-
value function of its respective dimension, and the overall joint
Q-value is obtained as the sum of the three components.
7) MLP-PPO: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) based
on MLP. MLP-PPO adopts an Actor–Critic architecture. An
Actor network outputs three independent discrete action dis-
tributions corresponding to frequency, time-slot, and power,
while a Critic network estimates the state value.
Fig.5 shows the mean and variance of the SINR for each al-
gorithm in 10 random scenarios. The proposed T-SAC achieves
the highest average SINR and the smallest variance in all
scenarios, indicating its optimal performance in interference

dimensional spaces due to its limited exploration capabilities,
resulting in poor performance in interference suppression. PPO
and the Random scheme have similar performance and are
the worst, indicating that PPO struggles to effectively explore
and learn optimal strategies in high-dimensional action space
scenarios, failing to demonstrate the learning capabilities of
reinforcement learning algorithms and only approaching the
level of random exploration. Overall, T-SAC improves the
average CINR by about 2–3 dB and reduces the variance by
more than 70% compared to the optimal baseline (MLP-SAC),
validating its global coordination capability.
Fig.6 shows T-SAC, in particular, exhibits an average SGM
exceeding 0.9 and a variance below 0.05, demonstrating
optimal resource utilization efficiency and strong robustness.
The average SGM of MLP-SAC is approximately 0.7–0.8,
while the average SGM of CNN-SAC, MLP-DQN, and the
Greedy scheme is approximately 0.6–0.7. The fixed and
random schemes have an average SGM of only about 0.5.
Experimental results demonstrate that learning algorithms can
learn the intrinsic mechanisms for improving resource allo-
cation fairness through policy optimization, thus significantly
outperforming non-learning algorithms in this dimension. Par-
ticularly, this indicates that T-SAC, through a self-attention
mechanism, achieves information modeling based on global
context, thereby realizing more efficient and fairer allocation
of spectrum resources and power.
Fig.7 shows the T-SAC algorithm maintains a high satis-
faction score close to 1.0 across all scenarios, with a variance
below 0.05, indicating its ability to stably meet the service
requirements of different beams under dynamic environment,
demonstrating excellent robustness and generalization ability.
The average satisfaction scores of MLP-SAC, greedy and
fixed scheme range from 0.85 to 0.9, while the average
satisfaction score of the CNN-SAC is approximately 0.8,
which can meet user needs to a certain extent. However, due
to the lack of global dependency modeling, it is difficult to
guarantee allocation fairness. The satisfaction scores of DQN,
PPO, and random scheme are all significantly low, reflecting
their difficulty in achieving effective resource coordination in
complex dynamic environments.
